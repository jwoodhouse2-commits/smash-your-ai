Mistakes we have made

So you do not have to make them yourself

Before we dive into the tools and techniques, we want to be honest about something. We have made every mistake in this book. Between us, James and Paul have over 40 years in education and have been using AI tools daily for the past two years. Here are the real lessons we learned the hard way.

Lesson 1

We trusted AI-generated facts without checking

Early on, we used AI to generate revision materials for students. The content sounded perfect. Professional, well-structured, and confident. Then a student pointed out that one of the "facts" was completely wrong. AI had invented a plausible-sounding definition that did not match the exam board's specification at all.

What we learned: AI does not know the difference between right and wrong. It only knows what sounds plausible. Every factual claim needs verifying, especially in education where students are relying on your materials to pass exams.

Lesson 2

We let AI get too creative

We asked AI to "write an engaging blog post about AI tools for small businesses." What we got back was full of hyperbole, metaphors, and phrases like "in today's rapidly evolving digital landscape" and "unleash the power of AI to transform your business." It sounded like a bad marketing brochure. We had to rewrite the whole thing.

What we learned: If you do not rein AI in, it defaults to corporate robot mode. You need explicit rules: no cliches, no filler phrases, no em dashes, write like a human. Custom instructions (which we covered in the first workbook) and custom GPTs (which we cover today) are how you solve this permanently.

Lesson 3

We did not spot a hallucinated citation

We asked AI to suggest some academic research to support a piece of content. It gave us three paper titles with author names, journal names, and publication years. They looked completely real. Two of the three papers did not exist. The author names were real researchers, but the specific papers were fabricated.

What we learned: AI does not retrieve citations. It generates them. It predicts what a citation should look like based on patterns. Section 4 of this workbook is dedicated to spotting and preventing this, because it is one of the most dangerous things AI does.

Lesson 4

We spent ages writing prompts that did not work

In the early days, we would spend 20 minutes crafting what we thought was the perfect prompt, only to get mediocre results. We would tweak a word here, add a sentence there, and try again. The results barely improved. We were doing prompt engineering the hard way.

What we learned: There is a better way. It is called meta prompting, and it completely changed how we work with AI. Instead of writing the prompt yourself, you get AI to write it for you. We dedicate a whole section to this later in the workbook (Section 6) because it is genuinely the single most powerful technique we have found.

Lesson 5

We tried to use one tool for everything

For months, we used ChatGPT for everything. Writing, research, fact-checking, image generation. It was fine at all of them but brilliant at none. Then we discovered that different tools are better at different things. NotebookLM for accuracy-critical research. Gemini for image generation. Claude for long-form writing. Perplexity for fact-checking.

What we learned: The best AI workflow uses multiple tools, each for what it does best. Today's workbook teaches you exactly which tool to use when.

The point of sharing these mistakes: AI is not magic. It is a powerful tool that requires skill to use well. The techniques in this workbook are what we wish we had known from day one. They will save you weeks of trial and error.

Golden nugget

The skill that separates casual users from power users

Knowing when NOT to use AI. The best AI users do not try to use it for everything. They know exactly what AI is good at and what it is terrible at. They have a mental checklist. And that clarity is what makes them fast, accurate, and trusted.

Before and after: the same task, two approaches

Without this skill

You ask AI: "Write me a report on our Q1 sales performance." AI generates a confident report full of invented statistics, made-up percentages, and fabricated comparisons to "industry benchmarks." You skim it, think it looks professional, and send it to your boss. Two days later, someone checks the numbers.

With this skill

You paste your actual Q1 data into AI and ask it to "Structure this data into a report format with headings, bullet points, and a summary. Do not add any numbers or claims that are not in the data I have provided." AI formats your real data beautifully. Every number is yours. Every claim is verified.

AI is brilliant at drafting, summarising, brainstorming, reformatting, and explaining. It is terrible at maths, precise facts, legal citations, recent events, and anything that requires lived experience. Understanding this boundary is the single most important skill you will develop today.

Understanding custom GPTs

Build AI that already knows your business

A custom GPT is a version of ChatGPT that you configure once and reuse forever. Instead of explaining your business, your tone, and your requirements every single conversation, you bake all of that into a custom GPT. It remembers everything. Think of it as creating a specialist employee who never forgets their briefing, never goes on holiday, and is available 24/7.

Why they matter: Consistent output every time. No re-explaining context. Shareable with your team. We have seen businesses cut their content creation time by 70% simply by building a custom GPT that knows their brand voice.

Not just ChatGPT: Google Gemini has an equivalent feature called Gems (free with Gemini), and Claude has Projects with custom instructions (free tier available, Pro $20/month). The principles in this section apply to all three. We will use ChatGPT's terminology, but everything translates directly.

The 5 components of a custom GPT

1

Name

Something descriptive. "AQA GCSE Physics Question Writer" not "My GPT 1".

2

Description

One sentence explaining what it does. Helps you find it later when you have 15 of them.

3

Instructions

The detailed prompt that tells it how to behave. This is the most important part.

4

Conversation starters

Pre-written prompts that appear when you open it. Saves typing and reminds you what it can do.

5

Knowledge files (documents)

PDFs, Word docs, spreadsheets you upload. The GPT references these in every conversation. This is a game-changer.

Uploading documents to custom GPTs

You can upload reference documents, and the GPT will use them to ground its answers. Upload your brand guidelines and the GPT writes content that follows them. Upload an exam spec and it generates questions that match it.

Uploading documents reduces hallucinations because the AI can reference your actual files instead of making things up. But here is the critical thing: it does not eliminate them entirely. Due to the way large language models work, they can still generate incorrect information even with reference documents. We cover verification techniques in detail in Section 4. NotebookLM (Section 3) goes even further with cited, page-referenced answers.

Common mistake: uploading too many documents. More is not better. 20 documents means the AI skims everything and misses details. Start with 3-5 of your most important documents. You can always add more later.

Template: writing custom GPT instructions

// Custom GPT instructions template

You are [role, e.g. "a professional email writer"]. You work for [company name], a [what your business does].

Your audience is [describe who you communicate with].

ALWAYS write in [tone, e.g. "a friendly, professional tone"].

ALWAYS use UK English.

ALWAYS keep responses [length preference, e.g. "under 200 words unless asked otherwise"].

ALWAYS [specific formatting rules, e.g. "use bullet points for lists"].

NEVER use em dashes, cliches, or filler phrases.

NEVER use words like "leverage," "synergy," or "utilize."

NEVER add information the user has not provided.

REFERENCE the uploaded documents when answering. Cite which document the information came from.

If the answer is NOT in the uploaded documents, say so clearly rather than guessing.

Common mistake: not iterating. Your first version will be about 60% right. That is normal. Expect to refine 3-4 times. After every session, ask yourself: "What did it get wrong?" Then update the instructions. The magic is in the tweaking.

Building your first custom GPT

Full walkthroughs with real example outputs

Time to build. Open ChatGPT and go to Explore GPTs (sidebar), then click Create. For Gemini Gems, go to Gem manager and click New Gem. For Claude Projects, click Projects and create a new one.

We are going to show three GPTs in full detail with example outputs. Read the prompts carefully, understand why each instruction is there, then adapt for your own use case.

Example 1: Teacher exam question generator

Upload your exam spec PDF and past papers as knowledge files.

// Exam question generator - full instructions

You are an expert exam question writer for [subject, e.g. "AQA GCSE Computer Science"]. You generate exam-style questions that match the specification and assessment objectives of the uploaded spec document.

When I give you a topic or spec reference, generate exam questions following these rules:

- Format each question with the question number and text on the left

- Show the number of marks in bold square brackets right-aligned, e.g. [4]

- After each question, include a bordered answer box sized for the marks (2 lines per mark for short answers, larger for extended response)

- For table questions, include a properly formatted table for students to complete

- After ALL questions, produce a separate mark scheme with mark allocation per point

ALWAYS check the uploaded specification to ensure questions are within scope.

ALWAYS match the style and difficulty of real exam papers.

ALWAYS include a mix of AO1 (knowledge), AO2 (application), and AO3 (analysis).

ALWAYS use command words from the specification (state, describe, explain, evaluate).

NEVER create questions on topics not in the uploaded specification.

If a topic is out of scope, tell me rather than making questions up.

Example output

1. Define the term "abstraction" in the context of computational thinking.

[2]

2. Explain two reasons why a programmer might use subroutines when writing a large program.

[4]

Mark scheme

1. Removing unnecessary detail (1) to focus on the important/relevant aspects of a problem (1)

2. Award 1 mark per reason + 1 mark per explanation, e.g.: Code reuse - the subroutine can be called multiple times without rewriting the code (1+1). Easier to debug - errors can be isolated to a specific subroutine (1+1).

Common mistake: trusting mark allocations blindly. AI sometimes allocates marks incorrectly. A 2-mark "explain" question might actually need 4 marks. Always cross-check against real past paper mark schemes.

Example 2: Estate agent listing writer

Upload your brand guidelines and examples of your best listings as knowledge files.

// Property listing writer - full instructions

You are a property listing writer for [agency name], an estate agency based in [location]. Our typical buyer is [describe].

When I give you property details, generate a complete listing with:

1. A compelling headline (max 10 words, no exclamation marks)

2. Key features as bullet points (max 8)

3. A descriptive overview (2-3 paragraphs, 150-200 words total)

4. A location paragraph highlighting nearby amenities

5. EPC rating note and standard disclaimer

ALWAYS write in present tense. ALWAYS use UK English and UK property terminology (garden not yard, lounge not living room).

NEVER claim distances or travel times unless I explicitly provide them.

NEVER use superlatives like "stunning" unless the property genuinely warrants it.

NEVER fabricate features, room counts, or square footage I have not provided.

NotebookLM masterclass

AI answers grounded in your own documents

NotebookLM is Google's tool for getting AI answers grounded in YOUR documents. Unlike ChatGPT or Claude, it does not draw on general knowledge. Every answer comes directly from your uploaded sources, with citations showing exact page and section references so you can verify where each answer came from.

If you set up document uploads in your custom GPTs in Section 2, you already understand the value of grounding AI in real documents. NotebookLM takes this further by making citations a first-class feature. Click any citation and see the exact passage. Free with any Google account.

Important caveat: NotebookLM significantly reduces hallucinations but is not 100% foolproof. It can still misinterpret passages or combine information incorrectly. Section 4 covers verification in depth.

4

Spotting and preventing hallucinations

Because AI never says "I do not know"

AI hallucinations are when the model generates confident-sounding nonsense. It will cite papers that do not exist. It will invent statistics. It will fabricate case law. And it does all of this with the same authoritative tone it uses when correct. It never warns you.

Why this happens: Large language models predict the most likely next word based on patterns. When they lack the right information, they do not stop. They generate what sounds plausible instead. This is not a bug. It is fundamental to how the technology works.

Real consequences

The lawyer who cited fake cases

In 2023, a New York lawyer submitted a court filing citing six legal cases. None existed. ChatGPT generated convincing case names, citations, and ruling summaries. The lawyer was sanctioned by the court. He told the judge he did not know AI could fabricate case law.

Fabricated academic references

University lecturers routinely catch students citing papers that do not exist. AI generates plausible author names, journal titles, and years. The references look real at a glance but fall apart when you search for them.

Invented business data

A marketing team used AI-generated "market research statistics" in a board presentation. The numbers sounded right and the trends made sense. But the specific figures were fabricated. They discovered this when a board member tried to find the source.

Red flags checklist

Specific numbers or statistics you did not provide
Citations to papers, books, or articles - always check they exist
Legal references or case law - AI will confidently cite non-existent cases
Claims about recent events - training data has a cutoff date
Mathematical calculations - always verify with a calculator
Overly detailed answers to vague questions - filling space with invention

The prevention toolkit

1. Use NotebookLM for factual work

As you learned in Section 3, answers are grounded in your documents with citations. Your first line of defence.

2. The "are you sure?" technique

After getting a response, pick the most specific claim and ask: "Are you sure about [claim]? Double-check and tell me your confidence level." This forces the model to re-evaluate rather than just generating the next plausible thing. It does not always catch errors, but it catches enough to be worth doing every time.

3. Ask for sources (and check them)

Add "Cite your sources for every factual claim" to your prompt. Then actually look them up.

4. Cross-reference with Perplexity

Perplexity AI (free, or Pro $20/month) searches the web and provides sources. Ask the same question there and compare.

5. Never trust numbers, dates, or legal references

Verify independently. Every time. No exceptions.

6. Build honesty rules into your GPTs

Add: "If you are not certain, say so clearly. Never fabricate information."

Common mistake: saying "check this" without criteria. AI will almost always say it looks great. It is a people-pleaser. Instead: "Check this against [specific criteria] and list every deviation. Only tell me what is wrong or missing."

Exercise: write a verification prompt

// Verification prompt template

[Your question here]

After answering:

1. Rate your confidence in each factual claim (1-10).

2. For anything below 8, explain why and suggest how I could verify it.

3. If you are not certain, say "I am not sure about this" rather than guessing.

Evening

Building real workflows

This is where everything comes together. Instead of using AI for one-off tasks, you are going to chain multiple steps into workflows that save hours every week. We will walk through a complete example, then you will design your own.

The difference between a tool and a workflow

A tool does one thing. A workflow connects multiple steps so the output of one becomes the input of the next. That is where the real time savings happen - not minutes, but hours.

Worked example: estate agent property listing workflow

Let's say you have just taken photos of a new property, written some rough notes during the viewing, and need to produce a full listing. Here is the complete workflow, step by step.

Step 1: Upload your photos and notes

Upload your viewing notes (handwritten or typed) plus 5-10 property photos to ChatGPT or Claude.

// Step 1 prompt

I am an estate agent. I have just finished a property viewing and need to create a listing. Here are my viewing notes and photos.

First, analyse everything I have uploaded and give me:

1. A summary of the property's key features

2. Anything you can see in the photos that I have not mentioned in my notes

3. Any questions I should answer before we write the listing

Example output:

Key features identified: 3-bed semi-detached, south-facing garden (visible from photo 3 - afternoon sun on lawn), recently fitted kitchen with integrated appliances, original Victorian fireplace in living room (photo 6), off-street parking for 2 cars.

From photos but not in your notes: The bathroom appears to have underfloor heating (visible thermostat in photo 8). The kitchen has a wine fridge built into the island unit. There is a garden office/shed at the rear.

Questions before writing: What is the asking price? EPC rating? Council tax band? Is the garden office heated/insulated? Any recent renovations beyond the kitchen?

Step 2: Generate the listing description

// Step 2 prompt

Based on everything above, write a property listing description. Use this format:

- Opening hook (one compelling sentence)

- Key details (beds, bathrooms, reception rooms, parking)

- Room-by-room walkthrough (ground floor then first floor)

- Outside space

- Location highlights (schools, transport, shops within 1 mile)

- Closing line with call to action

Tone: professional but warm. Avoid cliches like "must be seen to be believed" or "deceptively spacious". Maximum 300 words.

Step 3: Create social media versions

// Step 3 prompt

Now create three social media posts from this listing:

1. Facebook - conversational, 80-100 words, include emojis sparingly, end with "DM us or call [number]"

2. Instagram caption - lifestyle-focused, 60-80 words, include 10 relevant hashtags at the end

3. LinkedIn - professional market insight angle, 100-120 words, position us as local market experts

Step 4: Generate the email alert

// Step 4 prompt

Finally, write a short email to send to buyers on our mailing list who are looking for 3-bed properties in this area.

Subject line: short, curiosity-driven, no clickbait

Body: 50-80 words, highlight the 3 most appealing features, include a clear "Book a viewing" call to action.

Total time: roughly 15 minutes

That workflow - listing, 3 social posts, and an email - used to take 1-2 hours. The AI does not replace your expertise (you still check every detail), but it eliminates the blank page problem and handles the repetitive formatting.

Meta prompting

This is probably the single most powerful technique in this entire workbook. Meta prompting means asking the AI to help you write a better prompt before you use it. Instead of guessing what to ask, you let the AI interview you first.

Why we teach this

We used to spend ages crafting the "perfect" prompt from scratch. Then we realised: the AI already knows what makes a good prompt. So why not ask it? This one technique has transformed how we work - and how we teach. If you only take one thing from this workbook, make it this.

How it works: the four-step process

1

Describe what you want (roughly)

Tell the AI what you are trying to achieve - it does not need to be detailed or well-structured.

2

Ask the AI to interview you

It asks 5-10 detailed questions to understand your context, audience, constraints, and preferences.

3

Answer the questions

Your answers give the AI everything it needs to write a prompt you could never have written from scratch.

4

Get the optimised prompt - then use it

The AI produces a detailed, structured prompt. Copy it, open a new chat, and paste it in.

Why a new chat?

The chat where you built the prompt has lots of back-and-forth context. Starting fresh means the AI focuses entirely on executing the polished prompt without being influenced by the drafting conversation.

The meta prompt

This is the prompt you use to start the process. Memorise it or save it somewhere accessible.

// The meta prompt

I want you to help me write the perfect prompt for the following task:

[Describe your task in plain English - a few sentences is fine]

Before writing the prompt, ask me 10 in-depth questions about what I need. Cover things like:

- Who the output is for (audience)

- What tone and style I want

- Any constraints (word count, format, things to avoid)

- What a great result looks like vs a bad one

- Any context or background information you need from me

Ask the questions one at a time so we can have a conversation. Once you have all my answers, write the optimised prompt that I can copy and use in a new chat.

Worked example: teacher writing an end-of-term report

What the teacher typed:

"I want to write end-of-term reports for my Year 9 Science class. I have 30 students."

Some of the AI's questions:

  1. 1. What format does your school use - paragraph style, bullet points, or a template with sections?
  2. 2. What is the typical word count per student report?
  3. 3. Do you need to reference specific topics covered this term?
  4. 4. What data do you have for each student - grades, effort scores, specific strengths/weaknesses?
  5. 5. Are there any phrases your school requires or bans? (Some schools ban "could try harder")
  6. 6. Should the tone differ between high-achieving students and those who are struggling?
  7. 7. Do parents read these directly, or do they go through a head of year first?

The optimised prompt the AI produced:

// Generated prompt (for new chat)

You are an experienced UK secondary school Science teacher writing end-of-term reports for Year 9 students. Each report will be read directly by parents/carers.

For each student, I will provide: name, current grade (1-9), effort grade (A-D), two strengths, one area for improvement, and any specific incidents or achievements.

Write a 60-80 word report per student that:

- Opens with a positive, specific comment about their work this term

- References at least one of: atomic structure, forces, or ecosystems (topics covered)

- Uses the school's required structure: achievement, effort, target

- Avoids generic phrases like "good effort" or "could try harder"

- For students below grade 5, frames improvement supportively rather than critically

- Ends with a specific, actionable target for next term

I will provide the student details now. Write reports for all of them.

Common mistake: using the prompt in the same chat

The AI has just spent 10 messages interviewing you. If you use the prompt in the same chat, it might blend in old context and give inconsistent results. Always start a fresh chat for the final prompt.

Evening

Power features you should know about

These features take you from "using AI" to "getting the most out of AI". Each one is a genuine time-saver once you know it exists.

Chain prompting (building on previous outputs)

Instead of one massive prompt, break complex tasks into steps. Each message builds on the last. The AI remembers everything in the conversation.

// Example chain

Message 1: "Here is a transcript from today's team meeting. Summarise the key discussion points."

Message 2: "Now pull out every action item and who is responsible."

Message 3: "Format those action items as a table with columns: task, owner, deadline."

Message 4: "Write a follow-up email to the team with this table included. Keep it under 150 words."

This is exactly what we did in the workflow section (page 13) - but you can use chain prompting for any multi-step task.

Voice mode

ChatGPT's voice mode lets you have a spoken conversation with AI. It is surprisingly good for brainstorming, thinking through problems, or working when you cannot type.

Best uses for voice mode

  • - Brainstorming ideas while walking or driving
  • - Talking through a problem you are stuck on (rubber duck debugging)
  • - Practising presentations or difficult conversations
  • - Dictating content when typing feels slow

Cost: Voice mode is included with ChatGPT Plus (about $20/month, roughly £16/month). It is not available on the free plan.

Canvas (ChatGPT) and Artifacts (Claude)

These features open a side panel where the AI writes longer content - documents, code, articles - that you can edit directly. Think of it as a collaborative Google Doc.

When to use Canvas/Artifacts

  • - Writing anything longer than a few paragraphs
  • - Documents you want to iterate on (highlight a section and ask for changes)
  • - Code that you want to test and tweak
  • - Templates you plan to reuse

In ChatGPT, type "use canvas" to activate it. In Claude, artifacts appear automatically for longer outputs.

Projects (ChatGPT and Claude)

Projects let you group related conversations together and add persistent instructions and files. Every new chat in that project automatically has access to your uploaded documents and custom instructions.

// Example project setup

Project name: "Client Proposals"

Instructions: "You are a business development assistant for [company name]. Always use our brand voice (professional, friendly, no jargon). Proposals should follow our standard template structure."

Files uploaded: brand guidelines PDF, proposal template, pricing sheet, 3 example proposals

Now every conversation in this project can reference your brand guidelines, pricing, and past proposals without you uploading them each time.

How this connects to Custom GPTs: Projects are great for teams or for work that evolves over time. Custom GPTs (page 7) are better for specific, repeatable tasks. Use both.

Gems (Google Gemini)

Gems are Gemini's version of Custom GPTs. You give a Gem custom instructions and it behaves according to those instructions in every conversation. If you use Google Workspace (Gmail, Docs, Sheets), Gems can access your Google data directly.

Cost: Gems require Gemini Advanced ($19.99/month, roughly £16/month) as part of Google One AI Premium.

Sharing Custom GPTs with your team

Once you have built a Custom GPT that works well, you can share it with colleagues. In ChatGPT, choose "Anyone with the link" when publishing. Your team does not need to know how to prompt - they just use your GPT.

Watch out

Anyone you share with needs their own ChatGPT Plus subscription to use custom GPTs. The free plan does not support them.

AI image generation

Bonus section

AI can generate images from text descriptions. This is genuinely useful for social media graphics, presentations, blog headers, and product mockups - but it has limitations you need to understand.

The main tools

ChatGPT (DALL-E 3)

Easiest to use

Built into ChatGPT. Just describe what you want in plain English. Great for quick social media graphics, blog images, and illustrations.

Cost: Included with ChatGPT Plus ($20/month, ~£16/month). Limited generations on the free plan.

Best for: quick graphics, illustrations, social media images, concept visualisation

Google Gemini (Imagen 2)

Best free option

Google's image generator, available inside Gemini. Particularly good at photorealistic images and text rendering (getting words right in images).

Cost: Free tier available. Gemini Advanced ($19.99/month, ~£16/month) for higher limits and quality.

Best for: photorealistic images, images with text, product mockups

Midjourney

Most artistic

Produces the most visually stunning images. Runs through Discord or its own web app. Steeper learning curve but incredible results for creative work.

Cost: From $10/month (~£8/month). No free plan.

Best for: artistic/creative images, brand visuals, marketing materials, anything that needs to look premium

Our recommendation

Start with ChatGPT or Gemini - you likely already have access. They handle 80% of business image needs. Only consider Midjourney if you regularly need high-end creative visuals and are willing to learn its specific syntax.

Tips for better image prompts

1

Be specific about style: "a watercolour illustration of..." or "a clean, minimal flat design showing..." gives much better results than just describing the subject.

2

Mention the use case: "for a professional LinkedIn post" or "for a website hero banner, landscape ratio" helps the AI choose the right composition.

3

Iterate, do not start over: say "make the background lighter" or "remove the person on the left" rather than writing a completely new prompt.

4

Avoid text in images: AI still struggles with spelling. If you need text, add it afterwards in Canva or PowerPoint.

// Example image prompt

Create a clean, professional flat illustration for a blog post header. Subject: a small business owner sitting at a desk with a laptop, looking confident and productive. A friendly AI assistant is represented as a glowing teal orb floating beside the screen. Style: modern, minimal, warm lighting, white background. Landscape ratio (16:9). No text in the image.

Important: copyright and usage rights

AI-generated images sit in a legal grey area. You generally can use them for social media, blogs, and presentations. Be cautious using them for logos, trademarks, or anything you might need to defend legally. Check the specific terms of whichever tool you use.

The AI tools landscape

Your quick-reference guide to what is out there

There are hundreds of AI tools. You do not need most of them. Here are the ones that actually matter, grouped by what they do, with honest recommendations.

General-purpose AI assistants

These are your "do everything" tools. Pick one as your daily driver.

ChatGPT

Free / $20 mo (~£16)

Best all-rounder. Largest ecosystem (custom GPTs, plugins, voice mode). Our recommendation for most people starting out.

Claude

Free / $20 mo (~£16)

Excellent for long documents and nuanced writing. Handles up to 200,000 words of context. Often more careful and thoughtful than ChatGPT.

Google Gemini

Free / $19.99 mo (~£16)

Best for Google Workspace users. Can access your Gmail, Docs, and Drive directly. Strong image generation included.

Research and fact-finding

When you need current, sourced information.

Perplexity

Free / $20 mo (~£16)

AI-powered search engine. Gives sourced answers with citations. Brilliant for research, fact-checking, and finding current information.

NotebookLM

Free

Upload your own documents and ask questions about them. Only answers from your sources - never makes things up. See page 9 for our full guide.

Writing and content

Specialist tools for specific writing tasks.

Grammarly

Free / $12 mo (~£10)

Grammar, spelling, and tone checking everywhere you write. The AI features now rewrite and improve text. Works in email, Docs, social media.

Canva Magic Write

Free / $13 mo (~£10)

AI writing and image generation inside Canva. Great for social media posts, presentations, and marketing materials if you already use Canva.

Automation (connecting tools together)

Make your apps talk to each other without coding. These are covered in our Expert Level workbook.

Zapier / Make

Free tier / from $20 mo (~£16)

Connect apps together: "When I get an email with an attachment, save it to Google Drive and notify me on Slack." No coding required.

Speeding up your workflow

Type less, do more. Voice-to-text tools can dramatically speed up how you work.

Wispr Flow

From $8 mo (~£6.50)

Voice-to-text that works everywhere on your computer. Dictate emails, documents, messages, and prompts at the speed of speech. Learns your writing style over time. A genuine game-changer for productivity.

macOS / iOS Dictation

Free (built in)

Already on your Mac, iPhone, or iPad. Press the microphone key or enable Voice Control in Settings. Good enough for quick notes and messages.

Otter.ai

Free / $16.99 mo (~£14)

Records and transcribes meetings automatically. Joins your Zoom, Google Meet, or Teams calls and produces searchable transcripts with speaker labels.

Our tip: combine voice-to-text with AI. Dictate your rough thoughts using Wispr Flow or dictation, then paste them into ChatGPT or Claude to tidy up, restructure, or expand. It is the fastest way to go from idea to finished output.

Our honest advice

Start with one general-purpose tool (ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini) and one specialist (Perplexity for research or NotebookLM for document work). Master those before adding more. Most people get overwhelmed by trying too many tools at once.
